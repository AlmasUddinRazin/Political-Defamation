Political Defamation Cases in Bangladesh: Misuse of Defamation Laws, Challenges to Freedom of Expression, and the Need for Legal Reformation

Abstract: In Bangladesh, defamation remains a criminal offence under sections 499 and 500 of the Penal Code 1860, although most democratic systems treat it as a civil wrong. In political disputes, complaints can become pressure against opponents, journalists and critics rather than protection for genuine reputational harm. This paper examines how criminal defamation is politically misused and how reform can better protect freedom of expression under Article 39 of the Constitution. It uses a qualitative, doctrinal method, studying the Penal Code, the Code of Criminal Procedure 1898, the Constitution, cyber laws, reported judgments and five politically sensitive cases drawn from newspaper court reporting. It finds that the law already contains safeguards, notably the “person aggrieved” rule in section 198 of the Code of Criminal Procedure and the exceptions for truth and good-faith opinion, but they are weakly applied when a complaint is first accepted. Complaints are often filed by party workers rather than the person allegedly defamed, one statement can trigger cases in many districts, and cases can last years before ending in bail, stay, withdrawal or acquittal. The process itself becomes the penalty, although political motive cannot be proved from the cases alone. The paper recommends a review of sections 499 and 500, careful scrutiny of political complaints before proceedings continue, safeguards against repeated or baseless complaints, and faster trials.

Keywords: criminal defamation; political speech; freedom of expression; locus standi; Penal Code 1860; Bangladesh

1. Introduction

In recent years, criminal defamation laws in Bangladesh have frequently been used as a political tool rather than a shield to protect personal reputation. According to a study by the Centre for Governance Studies (CGS), out of 1,436 cases filed under the Digital Security Act 2018 between October 2018 and September 2023, a major portion involved defamation charges against journalists, political critics, and ordinary citizens. In many instances, people faced lawsuits even when the statement was not directed at the complainant. When a single Facebook post or political comment triggers multiple cases across different districts, citizens become afraid to speak freely.

In simple words, defamation means making a false statement that harms another person’s good reputation. In most democratic countries, defamation is treated as a civil wrong (tort), where the harmed person asks the court for financial compensation. However, in Bangladesh, defamation remains a criminal offence under Sections 499 and 500 of the Penal Code 1860, carrying a punishment of up to two years of imprisonment, a fine, or both.

A severe gap exists between constitutional guarantees and current legal practice. While Article 39 of the Constitution of Bangladesh guarantees freedom of speech and expression subject to “reasonable restrictions,” criminal defamation provisions—historically under the now-repealed Digital Security Act 2018 and Cyber Security Act 2023, and currently under Sections 499 and 500 of the Penal Code 1860—have been weaponized by powerful political actors to silence dissent. Although recent cyber law reforms (following the Cyber Security Ordinance 2025) removed criminal defamation from cyber legislation, the continued existence of criminal defamation under the Penal Code, alongside the fear of arrest warrants and lengthy trials, still discourages open political debate.

This paper examines how defamation laws are politically misused in Bangladesh and why further legal reform is necessary. Section 2 states the main problem. Sections 3 and 4 set out the research aim, objectives, and research questions. Section 5 explains the research method. Section 6 reviews the existing laws, cases and literature, while Section 7 discusses five political defamation cases and how they affect freedom of expression. Sections 8, 9 and 10 give recommendations for legal reform, the limitations of the research, and a conclusion.

2. Problem Statement

In theory, defamation law should protect a person’s reputation from false and harmful claims while allowing citizens to criticize the government and politicians freely. Under constitutional rules and international human rights standards, defamation laws should never be used to shield public figures from legitimate political criticism.

In practice, however, the criminal justice system is often used as a tool for political harassment. There are four main reasons for this. First, even after the removal of defamation from recent cyber laws, criminal defamation under the Penal Code 1860 still allows courts to issue arrest warrants and force accused persons to appear repeatedly before a trial even starts. Second, courts frequently accept cases filed by third-party political activists on behalf of leaders, leading to multiple coordinated lawsuits across the country for a single comment. Third, severe court backlogs mean that even a false case can hang over a journalist’s or critic’s head for years, damaging their career and personal life. Fourth, the high cost of fighting criminal cases in multiple districts intimidates ordinary citizens from expressing political opinions.

The result is a serious gap between what the Constitution promises and what actually happens in court. Instead of providing fair justice to people whose reputations were truly harmed, the legal process itself is used to punish critics. This fear forces journalists and citizens into self-censorship, which weakens democracy. This paper studies this gap and asks how Bangladesh’s defamation laws can be reformed to prevent political misuse.

3. Research Aim and Objectives

Research Aim: The aim of this research is to examine how criminal defamation laws are politically misused in Bangladesh and how legal reforms can better protect freedom of expression.

Research Objectives:

  1. To explain the current legal framework of defamation in Bangladesh, focusing on Sections 499 and 500 of the Penal Code 1860 and recent cyber law reforms.
  2. To analyze the patterns of political misuse of criminal defamation cases against journalists, activists, and political opponents.
  3. To identify the procedural and legal loopholes—such as jail punishments and third-party case filings—that allow this misuse under Article 39 of the Constitution.
  4. To recommend clear legal and procedural reforms to stop political harassment while ensuring fair remedies for genuine victims.

4. Research Questions

Main Research Question: How do Bangladeshi statutory provisions and judicial decisions balance the protection of political reputation against the constitutional right to freedom of speech and expression in political defamation cases?

To answer this central inquiry systematically and align each line of analysis with the paper’s core objectives, this study addresses four specific sub-questions:

  1. Substantive Elements and Exceptions: What essential elements must a complainant establish to succeed in a political defamation proceeding under Sections 499–502 of the Penal Code 1860, and how broadly do Bangladeshi courts apply the statutory exceptions for public good and good-faith criticism of public conduct?
  2. Procedural Safeguards and Locus Standi: How does the “person aggrieved” requirement under Section 198 of the Code of Criminal Procedure 1898 operate in political defamation cases, particularly when party activists or third parties file complaints on behalf of political leaders?
  3. Impact of Digital and Cyber Legislation: How have special cyber statutes—from the Information and Communication Technology Act 2006 and the Digital Security Act 2018 to the Cyber Security Act 2023 and subsequent cyber law reforms up to 2026—altered the prosecution and chilling effect of political speech in Bangladesh?
  4. Constitutional Proportionality and Reform: What judicial safeguards and statutory reforms are necessary in Bangladesh to prevent the filing of multiple, politically motivated defamation suits while preserving legitimate remedies for reputational harm under Article 39 of the Constitution?

5. Research Method and Methodology

This study adopts a qualitative, doctrinal (black-letter) legal research methodology. Doctrinal research systematically examines statutory texts, constitutional provisions, judicial precedents, and domestic legal scholarship to determine what the existing law is, identify internal inconsistencies, and evaluate whether current legal rules operate fairly in practice. A doctrinal approach is well suited to this topic because the central problems surrounding political defamation in Bangladesh arise from the wording of penal and cyber statutes, procedural rules on cognizance, and how the Supreme Court of Bangladesh interprets constitutional free-speech guarantees.

The research relies exclusively on Bangladeshi legal sources divided into two categories:

Primary Sources: Primary legal authorities comprise the Constitution of the People’s Republic of Bangladesh (specifically Article 39), the Penal Code 1860 (Sections 499–502), the Code of Criminal Procedure 1898 (Section 198 and the 2011 amendment substituting summons for arrest warrants in defamation cases), the Information and Communication Technology Act 2006 (repealed Section 57), the Digital Security Act 2018 (repealed Sections 25 and 29), the Cyber Security Act 2023, the Cyber Security Ordinance 2025, and the Cyber Security Act 2026. Primary sources also include reported judgments of the Supreme Court of Bangladesh published in the Dhaka Law Reports (DLR), Bangladesh Legal Decisions (BLD), Bangladesh Law Chronicles (BLC), and the Supreme Court Online Bulletin (SCOB).

Secondary Sources: Secondary authorities consist of peer-reviewed articles published in Bangladeshi law journals—such as the Dhaka University Law Journal and the Bangladesh Journal of Law—alongside domestic legal commentaries, human rights documentation on speech-related prosecutions, and investigative reporting from leading Bangladeshi national dailies, including The Daily Star, Prothom Alo, and Dhaka Tribune.

Selection Criteria and Limitations: Court decisions were purposively selected from DLR, BLD, BLC, and SCOB because they represent binding or persuasive precedents of the High Court Division and Appellate Division interpreting Section 499 of the Penal Code, Section 198 of the Code of Criminal Procedure, and Article 39 of the Constitution in cases involving public officials, journalists, and political figures. Domestic journal articles and newspaper reports were chosen to document both doctrinal debates and the real-world pattern of coordinated defamation filings across multiple districts. Because this study is strictly jurisdictional and examines internal Bangladeshi statutory and judicial developments, it does not undertake a multi-country comparative survey. Furthermore, this research involves no interviews, surveys, or empirical fieldwork; it relies solely on published legal and journalistic materials available up to November 2026.

6. Literature Review

6.1 Constitutional Framework and the Substantive Law of Defamation in Bangladesh

In Bangladesh, the law of defamation operates at the intersection of constitutional free-speech protection and colonial-era penal statutes. Article 39(1) of the Constitution guarantees freedom of thought and conscience, while Article 39(2) guarantees freedom of speech, expression, and the press, subject to “reasonable restrictions” imposed by law in the interests of state security, public order, decency, contempt of court, or defamation (Constitution of Bangladesh 1972, art 39). In State v Chief Editor, Manabzamin, the High Court Division reaffirmed that while press freedom is essential to democracy, Article 39(2) does not grant a licence to publish malicious, libellous, or false imputations that destroy individual reputation (57 DLR (HCD) 359).

Although defamation is actionable as a civil tort for damages in Bangladesh, political defamation disputes are overwhelmingly pursued as criminal offences under Chapter XXI of the Penal Code 1860 (Rahman, 2026). Under Section 499, defamation requires making or publishing an imputation concerning a person—by words spoken or intended to be read, signs, or visible representations—with the intention to harm, or knowledge or reason to believe that it will harm, that person’s reputation, punishable under Section 500 with simple imprisonment up to two years, a fine, or both (Penal Code 1860, ss 499–500). In Khwaja Noor-Uddin v Hamidul Huq Chowdhury, the court observed that whether words lower a person’s estimation must be judged by the standards of ordinary, prudent members of society, taking into account the public standing of the person defamed (16 DLR 1964). Similarly, in A.K.M. Enamul Haque v Md. Mizanur Rahman, the High Court Division quashed a defamation proceeding on the ground that merely defiant or disrespectful utterances made during a dispute, without specific intent to assassinate character, do not satisfy Section 499 (14 BLD (HCD) 201).

Crucially, Section 499 contains ten statutory exceptions designed to protect public debate. The First Exception protects imputations of truth made or published for the “public good,” while the Second and Third Exceptions protect good-faith opinions regarding the conduct of public servants and public questions. In Khondokar Abu Taleb v State, the Supreme Court held that in a criminal prosecution under Section 499, an accused is entitled to acquittal if they demonstrate that the imputation was substantially true (19 DLR (SC) 198). Applying the public-interest exceptions in Shahadat Chowdhury v Md. Ataur Rahman, the High Court Division refused to find criminal malice where a journalist reported allegations of corruption against a senior Roads and Highways engineer, holding that good-faith scrutiny of public officials serves the public interest (25 CLC (HCD) 1996). Likewise, in Md. Abdun Noor v State (50 DLR (HCD) 456) and Abdul Hamid v Habib Ahmed (4 BLC (HCD) 343), the High Court Division quashed prosecutions where complaints or statements fell squarely within good-faith exceptions. While these precedents articulate sound substantive protections, Bangladeshi scholars observe that the burden of proving an exception falls on the accused during trial, meaning political critics must still endure prolonged criminal proceedings before invoking these defences (Mostofa, 2026).

6.2 Locus Standi, Cognizance, and Multi-District Harassment in Political Cases

A recurring theme in Bangladeshi legal literature is the procedural weaponisation of criminal defamation against politicians, journalists, and civil society figures. Unlike civil defamation suits—which require payment of ad valorem court fees and are subject to a one-year limitation period under Articles 24 and 25 of the Limitation Act 1908—criminal complaints before Magistrate courts entail minimal filing costs (Rahman, 2026). Although Parliament amended the Code of Criminal Procedure in 2011 so that magistrates issue summons rather than immediate arrest warrants in defamation complaints, the filing of multiple simultaneous cases across different districts remains a potent tool of harassment (The Daily Star, 2016).

To prevent busybodies from instituting proxy litigation, Section 198 of the Code of Criminal Procedure 1898 mandates that no court shall take cognizance of an offence under Chapter XXI of the Penal Code except upon a complaint made by “some person aggrieved by such offence” (CrPC 1898, s 198). In Mahmudur Rahman v Bangladesh and Others, the High Court Division examined the scope of Section 198 and double-jeopardy protections under Article 35 of the Constitution when multiple complaints were lodged over the same publication (9 SCOB [2017] HCD 119). More recently, in Tasnova Iqbal v The State, the High Court Division quashed cognizance in a defamation proceeding, reiterating that strict compliance with locus standi under Section 198 and prima facie satisfaction of the ingredients of Section 499 are mandatory preconditions before summoning an accused (20 SCOB [2025] HCD 274).

Despite this clear statutory rule, Bangladeshi newspaper archives and legal analyses document how local political activists routinely file dozens of defamation complaints in subordinate courts nationwide whenever a national political leader or editor criticises a rival party (Prothom Alo, 2016). For instance, in 2016, 83 lawsuits—comprising 66 defamation cases and 17 sedition cases—were filed across 53 districts against The Daily Star editor Mahfuz Anam by ruling-party activists, forcing the High Court Division to intervene and stay the proceedings (Dhaka Tribune, 2016). Similarly, politically motivated defamation cases filed in 2010 against Professor Muhammad Yunus remained pending for over a decade until the High Court Division scrapped the proceedings and the Appellate Division upheld that quashment in July 2025 (The Daily Star, 2025). These episodes demonstrate a sharp disconnect between superior court jurisprudence on locus standi and the routine admission of proxy political complaints by trial magistrates.

6.3 The Expansion of Political Defamation into Cyber Legislation (2006–2026)

The migration of political debate onto social media and online news portals prompted successive Bangladeshi governments to enact special cyber statutes that dramatically expanded the reach of defamation law. Initially, Section 57 of the Information and Communication Technology Act 2006 criminalised the electronic publication of fake, obscene, or defamatory material, prescribing severe custodial sentences and making the offence cognizable and non-bailable (ICT Act 2006, s 57). Following widespread criticism from Bangladeshi legal scholars and journalists, Section 57 was repealed and replaced by the Digital Security Act 2018 (DSA), which retained criminal defamation under Section 29 alongside broadly worded offences in Sections 25 and 31 (DSA 2018, ss 25, 29).

Bangladeshi academic literature highlights two major structural flaws in the DSA 2018 regime as applied to political speech. First, unlike Section 198 of the CrPC, which restricted standing to an “aggrieved person,” cyber offences under the DSA allowed police officers and third-party political supporters to lodge First Information Reports directly, resulting in warrantless arrests and prolonged pre-trial detention (Bari, 2023; Karim, 2022). Second, vague statutory terms such as “tarnishing the image of the state” or publishing “offensive or false” information blurred the constitutional boundary between legitimate political dissent and unlawful defamation (Hossain, 2020).

Subsequent legislative reforms reflect ongoing tension over how online political speech should be regulated. The Cyber Security Act 2023 abolished imprisonment for cyber defamation under Section 29, replacing it with a maximum fine of Tk 25 lakh, yet scholars noted that heavy fines and the retention of Section 25 still chilled political expression (Mostofa, 2026). Following the political transition of 2024, the Cyber Security Ordinance 2025 and the Cyber Security Act 2026 omitted standalone cyber defamation provisions and annulled pending speech-related proceedings under Section 50(4), returning online defamation claims to the traditional Penal Code framework (Rahman, 2026). However, legal commentators warn that draft amendments circulated in late 2026 proposing new penalties for “rumours” and “insulting” content risk reviving executive overreach against political critics (Prothom Alo, 2026).

6.4 Absence of a Differentiated Public-Figure Standard

A critical doctrinal observation in recent Bangladeshi scholarship is that domestic statutory law treats political leaders and private citizens identically when adjudicating reputational injury. Writing in the Dhaka University Law Journal, Mostofa argues that Bangladeshi law lacks a clear “public figure doctrine” requiring politicians and public officials to meet a higher fault threshold—such as proving actual malice or reckless disregard for truth—when suing over criticism of their public duties (Mostofa, 2026). While the High Court Division in Shahadat Chowdhury v Md. Ataur Rahman recognised that public officials must tolerate good-faith scrutiny of their official conduct (25 CLC (HCD) 1996), this principle has not been codified into a structured constitutional standard binding on subordinate magistrates. As a result, trial courts frequently treat sharp political rhetoric, satire, and investigative reporting as prima facie criminal defamation.

6.5 The Gap in the Literature

Existing Bangladeshi legal literature thoroughly analyses the statutory ingredients of Section 499 of the Penal Code, critiques the unconstitutional overreach of the repealed ICT Act 2006 and Digital Security Act 2018, and documents individual instances of judicial quashment by the High Court Division. However, two significant gaps remain. First, very few studies synthesize the post-2025 cyber law reforms (the Cyber Security Ordinance 2025 and the Cyber Security Act 2026) with traditional Penal Code and Section 198 CrPC jurisprudence to evaluate how political defamation operates now that online speech offences have reverted to the Penal Code. Second, existing scholarship rarely connects procedural abuse at the magistrate court level—specifically third-party proxy complaints and multi-district forum shopping—with the absence of a formal public-figure standard under Article 39 of the Constitution. This research paper fills that gap by examining substantive rules, procedural standing, and recent statutory reforms together to propose concrete safeguards for political speech in Bangladesh.

7. Discussion and Findings

7.1 Case selection and approach

This section tests the research questions in Section 4 against five cases in which political speech, or a politically charged complaint, led to defamation proceedings, involving an editor, opposition leaders, an editor-politician and a government officer. The reported judgments in Section 6 set the legal standard. The cases themselves come from court reporting in national newspapers, because we found no reported judgments for them, so most court actions below are bail, stay or cognisance orders, not final judgments. We found no case or writ numbers. Reported facts, court action and our analysis are kept separate.

7.2 Case 1: Mahfuz Anam, editor of The Daily Star (2016)

Case and citation. Writ petition of Mahfuz Anam, High Court Division; stay order of 11 April 2016.

Facts. After Anam said on television in February 2016 that his paper had printed unverified military-intelligence material in 2007–08, party members, mostly Awami League and its student and youth wings, filed complaints in dozens of districts (Prothom Alo, 2016). His lawyer counted 83 cases (66 defamation, 17 sedition); sources differ (BenarNews, 2016).

Issues and law. Can one publication be prosecuted in many courts, and were the complainants “persons aggrieved” (CrPC s 198)?

Decision and reasoning. The High Court Division stayed 72 cases for three months and asked the government to justify them. Counsel argued that only the aggrieved person may complain and that travelling to courts nationwide was unrealistic at his age (Dhaka Tribune, 2016). We found no final decision.

Our analysis. Our argument is that the harm is structural. Each private complaint needed its own bail and hearing, so the burden of defence became the penalty even if every case failed. The complainants may fairly say that an admitted lapse can offend. But Sheikh Hasina herself was not the complainant in any filing we found; one complaint by her would have sufficed.

7.3 Case 2: Khaleda Zia’s Liberation War remarks (2015–2024)

Case and citation. Complaints against Khaleda Zia, Narail (December 2015) and Dhaka (January 2016).

Facts. On 21 December 2015 she said at a Dhaka discussion that the number of Liberation War martyrs was disputed. Complaints followed, including one by AB Siddique of Jananetri Parishad, a political group; case papers also alleged remarks about Sheikh Mujibur Rahman (The Independent, 2018; The Shillong Times, 2018).

Issues and law. Is doubting a historical figure defamation, and who is aggrieved when those said to be defamed are unnamed martyrs and a deceased leader (CrPC s 198)?

Decision and reasoning. She was granted bail in 2018 and 2022, and on 3 September 2024 was acquitted in five defamation cases from the Awami League period, including cases over remarks about the Liberation War (The Daily Star, 2022; The Daily Star, 2025). The reports give no reasons.

Our analysis. Our argument is that criminal law was used to settle a historical debate that evidence, not a warrant, should answer. A complainant must show whose reputation was harmed (section 499, Explanations 1 and 2) and why he may complain (section 198); the reports do not show this was tested. Supporters say doubting the figure insults the dead, a serious moral point but not legal standing. Acquittal after almost nine years does not itself prove motive; it shows the process was the penalty.

7.4 Case 3: Mirza Fakhrul Islam Alamgir, “killer party” remarks (2014–2017)

Case and citation. Complaint by SM Nur-e-Alam Siddique against Mirza Fakhrul Islam Alamgir, Dhaka, filed 1 September 2014.

Facts. At a press conference on 24 August 2014 Fakhrul called the Awami League a “killer party” and Sheikh Hasina a “killer”. A week later the vice-president of the ruling party’s fishermen’s wing filed the complaint (Dhaka Tribune, 2014).

Issues and law. Is harsh political rhetoric fact or opinion, and can a magistrate act on a complaint from someone not personally defamed (CrPC s 198)?

Decision and reasoning. In July 2017 a Dhaka magistrate framed charges, which The Daily Star reported as ignoring the section 198 bar; Fakhrul’s party said it would seek quashing (The Daily Star, 2017). We found no reasons and no final outcome.

Our analysis. This is the clearest failure of the section 198 filter: a party office-bearer cannot stand in for the person said to be defamed. The other side is that “killer” is a grave accusation, and Fakhrul could raise truth and public good (Exception 1), but only at a trial lasting years. A separate 2019 complaint by AB Siddique over remarks in London ended in October 2024 with Fakhrul and Tarique Rahman acquitted under CrPC section 247, which concerns a complainant who does not appear, so the speech was never judged (Dhaka Tribune, 2024).

7.5 Case 4: Mahmudur Rahman, acting editor of Amar Desh (2017–2018)

Case and citation. Complaints against Mahmudur Rahman in many districts from December 2017.

Facts. At a National Press Club seminar on 1 December 2017 he reportedly called Bangladesh “a colony of India” and said Sheikh Mujib destroyed democracy and the press. Within days party activists filed complaints in many districts, one seeking Tk 1,000 crore and some adding sedition-type sections (123A, 124A, 505) (Dhaka Tribune, 2017; The Daily Star, 2017).

Issues and law. Can one speech support complaints in many districts, and was each complainant aggrieved (CrPC s 198)? Section 6.2 reports that the High Court Division examined this and double jeopardy in Mahmudur Rahman v Bangladesh (9 SCOB [2017] HCD 119).

Decision and reasoning. A Kushtia magistrate granted permanent bail after counsel argued that the complainant was not aggrieved; Chhatra League men then allegedly attacked him at the court. The High Court Division later granted six months’ bail in a defamation case (The Daily Star, 2018). No final outcomes found.

Our analysis. First, adding sedition-type sections turns a speech dispute into a state-security case with heavier penalties. Second, most of what he said was opinion on public questions, which Exceptions 2 and 3 to section 499 protect when made in good faith; a court should test that at the start. In fairness, personal remarks about the Prime Minister’s family are not protected the same way and should be separated from political opinion.

7.6 Case 5: Gazi Tariq Salman, Barguna Upazila Nirbahi Officer (2017)

Case and citation. Complaint by Obaidullah Sazu against Gazi Tariq Salman, Barisal, 7 June 2017.

Facts. A local lawyer and Awami League leader sued the officer for printing a child’s portrait of Sheikh Mujib on an invitation card, calling it an insult. The magistrate accepted the complaint, and on 19 July 2017 the officer was sent to jail when he surrendered to seek bail, and released hours later (Dhaka Tribune, 2017; The Daily Star, 2017).

Issues and law. Was the complainant aggrieved (CrPC s 198), and can a tribute portrait amount to an imputation at all (Penal Code s 499, Explanation 1)?

Decision and reasoning. After public criticism the party suspended the complainant, who withdrew the case; the court allowed it. The Prime Minister herself praised the officer’s initiative (The Daily Star, 2017). There was no ruling on the merits. The magistrate said bail was delayed, not refused.

Our analysis. First, the claim fails on basic law: under Explanation 1 to section 499, an imputation against a dead person is defamation only if it would harm his reputation had he lived and is meant to hurt his family’s feelings; a tribute makes no imputation, and the complainant was not family. Second, the target was a serving officer, so the weakness is in the gatekeeping, not one party. Third, the cure was political intervention, not legal process; a safeguard that works only for the person who catches the Prime Minister’s attention is not a safeguard (The Daily Star, 2017).

7.7 Comparative analysis

Four patterns appear across the five cases.

1. The complainant is rarely the person said to be defamed. In none of the five did the reports show the named person as complainant, contrary to section 198, which the High Court Division has restated (Tasnova Iqbal v The State, 20 SCOB [2025] HCD 274). The rule is clear; the failure is at the trial court’s gate, as Section 6.2 observed.

2. The process punishes before judgment does: bail, stays and years of delay, but no reported conviction in these matters.

3. The legal defences are rarely reached, so no case law develops on where criticism ends and defamation begins (Section 6.1).

4. Motive cannot be proved. Complainants were mostly party activists and the acquittals we found followed the 2024 change of government, but both facts can be read two ways. The pattern is a serious concern about misuse, not proof of bad faith in every complaint.

7.8 Summary of findings

  1. (Sub-question 1) The exceptions in section 499 protect truth and good-faith opinion, but cases end before they are tested, so critics carry the burden of the process.
  2. (Sub-question 2) Third-party complaints were accepted despite section 198, and one statement produced complaints in many districts.
  3. (Sub-question 3) All five cases arose under the Penal Code, so they do not test the cyber laws directly; if online defamation now falls under sections 499–500, as Section 6.3 reports, the same weaknesses will affect online speech.
  4. (Sub-question 4) Reform should focus on gatekeeping at the start: checking standing and the exceptions before cognisance, filtering repeated complaints, keeping sedition-type sections out of speech disputes, faster trials and reasoned, reported judgments.

8. Recommendations

Based on the findings of this research, it is clear that Bangladesh needs a more balanced approach to political defamation. Protecting an individual’s reputation is important, but the law should not become a tool for discouraging political disagreement or silencing people who question those in power. The following recommendations may help establish a fairer legal environment.

1. Review and reform the existing defamation laws
The government should review Sections 499 and 500 of the Penal Code 1860 to ensure that their application is consistent with democratic principles and freedom of expression. The law should clearly distinguish between genuine defamatory statements and ordinary political criticism, disagreement or opinions. Criminal proceedings should not be encouraged merely because a politician feels offended by someone’s statement.

2. Prevent the misuse of defamation cases for political purposes
Courts should carefully examine the facts and circumstances of politically sensitive defamation complaints before allowing proceedings to continue. Particular attention should be given to whether the complaint concerns actual damage to reputation or is being used to create pressure on political opponents, journalists or critics. Appropriate safeguards should also be introduced against repeated or knowingly baseless complaints.

3. Protect freedom of speech and expression
Article 39 of the Constitution of Bangladesh guarantees freedom of speech and expression, subject to certain reasonable restrictions, including defamation. Therefore, the government and judiciary should maintain a proper balance between protecting personal reputation and preserving the public’s right to discuss political matters. Citizens, journalists and opposition members should be able to question government decisions and criticise political leaders without unnecessary fear of legal harassment.

4. Ensure faster and fairer judicial proceedings
Political defamation cases should be resolved within a reasonable period. Unnecessary delays, repeated hearings and prolonged legal procedures can create financial and psychological pressure on the accused, even before a final judgment is delivered. Courts should follow effective case-management procedures and avoid unnecessary delays while ensuring that both parties receive a fair opportunity to present their arguments.

5. Strengthen judicial independence and accountability
Judicial independence is essential when dealing with politically sensitive cases. Courts should make decisions based on evidence, applicable laws and legal principles rather than political influence or public pressure. The judicial process should also remain transparent so that citizens can maintain confidence in the fairness of the justice system.

6. Increase public awareness and encourage responsible political communication
The government, educational institutions, media organisations and civil society should take initiatives to educate citizens about defamation, freedom of expression and responsible use of social media. Political leaders and public figures should also demonstrate tolerance towards criticism. At the same time, individuals should avoid spreading unverified allegations or deliberately publishing false information that may seriously damage another person’s reputation.

9. Limitations of the Research

This research has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the study is mainly based on qualitative and doctrinal research. It examines existing laws, judicial decisions, academic writings, newspaper reports and other published materials. It does not include direct interviews with judges, lawyers, journalists, politicians or individuals who have been involved in political defamation cases. Therefore, the research may not fully reflect the personal experiences of those affected by such proceedings.

Second, the research focuses mainly on selected political defamation cases in Bangladesh rather than examining every defamation case filed in the country. As a result, the findings may not represent the complete situation of defamation litigation throughout Bangladesh.

Third, obtaining reliable and comprehensive information about politically sensitive cases can be difficult. Some cases receive extensive media attention, while others receive very limited coverage. Newspaper reports may also present different interpretations of the same incident. Consequently, the research depends on the availability and reliability of published information.

Fourth, the legal and political environment of Bangladesh is constantly changing. Amendments, new legislation and changes in judicial interpretation may affect how defamation laws are applied. Therefore, some observations may require further review as the legal framework develops.

Future research could address these limitations by conducting interviews with legal professionals, journalists and affected individuals, examining a larger number of court decisions, and studying how political defamation proceedings affect freedom of expression in everyday life.

10. Conclusion

This research examined political defamation cases in Bangladesh, focusing on the relationship between the protection of individual reputation and the constitutional right to freedom of speech and expression. It also explored how defamation laws are applied in politically sensitive situations and whether their application can create pressure on political opponents, journalists and ordinary citizens.

The research demonstrates that defamation law has an important purpose: protecting individuals against statements that unlawfully damage their reputation. Sections 499 and 500 of the Penal Code 1860 provide the legal framework for criminal defamation, while Article 39 of the Constitution recognises freedom of speech and expression subject to reasonable restrictions. However, the existence of these legal protections does not automatically guarantee a fair balance between reputation and free expression.

The examination of selected cases also highlights the importance of considering how defamation proceedings are initiated, conducted and concluded. Some politically sensitive cases have remained in the judicial process for years before being dismissed. Such developments raise important questions about access to justice, procedural fairness and the possible consequences of prolonged litigation. However, the dismissal of a case on procedural grounds should not automatically be treated as proof that the original complaint was politically motivated.

Ultimately, the research suggests that Bangladesh needs a legal system in which genuine reputational harm is addressed without creating unnecessary restrictions on political discussion and public criticism. Reviewing existing laws, preventing the misuse of legal proceedings, strengthening judicial independence and increasing public awareness are important steps towards achieving this balance.

In conclusion, democracy does not require political leaders to be free from criticism; rather, it requires criticism to be expressed responsibly and the law to be applied fairly. Protecting reputation and protecting freedom of expression should not be treated as opposing goals. Both are essential for establishing a just, accountable and democratic Bangladesh.
